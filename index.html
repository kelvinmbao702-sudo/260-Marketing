Topic 1 · Introduction to Marketing

The foundation of all marketing thinking — definitions, concepts, and the evolution of how businesses approach the market.

📖 Core Definition

Philip Kotler: Marketing is "a social and managerial process by which individuals and groups obtain what they need and want through creating, offering, and exchanging products of value with others."

Simple version: Finding out what people want → creating it → getting it to them → communicating its value.

1.1 Role & Scope
What Marketing Does
  • Identifies customer needs and wants
  • Creates products/services that satisfy those needs
  • Sets pricing, distribution and promotion
  • Builds long-term customer relationships
  • Generates profit for the organisation
1.2 Marketing Concepts
5 Management Philosophies
  • Production – focus on efficiency & availability
  • Product – focus on quality & features
  • Selling – focus on aggressive promotion
  • Marketing – focus on customer needs
  • Societal – balance customer + society needs
1.3 Evolution
How Marketing Changed
  • 1800s: Production era — "make it, sell it"
  • 1920s: Sales era — persuasion & advertising
  • 1950s: Marketing era — customer-first thinking
  • 1980s: Relationship marketing — retain customers
  • 2000s+: Digital & data-driven marketing

Problem Recognition → Information Search → Evaluation of Alternatives → Purchase Decision → Post-Purchase Behaviour

Marketing aims to influence this process at every stage.

4.0 Influencing Factors
What Affects Buyer Behaviour

  • B2C: individual consumers

Topic 6 · Pricing Strategies & Programmes

Price is the only P that generates revenue — all others create costs. Getting it right is critical.

💰 Why Pricing Matters

Price signals quality, affects demand, and determines profitability. A 1% improvement in price can improve profit by 11%, making pricing the most powerful profit lever (McKinsey research).

6.0 Setting the Price
Steps in Pricing
  • Select the pricing objective
  • Determine demand (price elasticity)
  • Estimate costs (fixed + variable)
  • Analyse competitors' prices
  • Select pricing method
  • Select final price
Pricing Objectives
What Are We Trying to Achieve?
  • Survival — cover costs when demand is low
  • Maximum profit — find the profit-maximising point
  • Maximum market share — penetration pricing
  • Market skimming — high price for early adopters
  • Product-quality leadership — premium positioning
6.1 Adapting Prices
Price Adaptation Strategies
  • Geographical pricing (vary by location)
  • Discount & allowance pricing
  • Promotional pricing (loss leaders, special events)
  • Discriminatory pricing (different customers, different prices)
  • Product-mix pricing (bundle, optional, captive)
Price Changes
Initiating & Responding
  • Initiating cuts: gain market share, excess capacity
  • Initiating increases: cost inflation, excess demand
  • Responding to competitor cuts: maintain, reduce, or reposition
  • Price wars — dangerous for all participants

Topic 9 · Dealing with Competition

Understanding the competitive landscape and how to build strategies that outperform rivals while adapting to change.

⚔️ Two Ways to View Competition

  • Industry Concept: Competitors are businesses selling similar products in the same industry (e.g., all soft drink companies)
  • Market Concept: Competitors are anyone satisfying the same customer need (e.g., Coca-Cola competes with water, juice, coffee, etc.)
9.1 Competitive Analysis
Analysing Competitors
  • Identify who your competitors are
  • Assess their objectives, strategies & strengths
  • Estimate their reactions to your moves
  • Porter's 5 Forces framework
  • Competitive intelligence gathering
Competitive Positions
4 Market Roles
  • Market Leader — largest market share (defend)
  • Market Challenger — #2 or #3, attacks leader
  • Market Follower — copies leader safely
  • Market Nicher — serves small specialist segments
9.2 Competitive Strategies
Porter's Generic Strategies
  • Cost Leadership — be the cheapest producer
  • Differentiation — be unique, charge premium
  • Focus — serve a narrow market excellently
  • Stuck in the middle = danger zone
9.2 How Business is Changing
Adapting to Change
  • Globalisation — competing worldwide
  • Digital disruption — e-commerce, social media
  • Customer power — informed, demanding buyers
  • Companies respond: CRM, innovation, agility
  • Sustainability & CSR demands increasing
Porter's 5 Forces

🏭 Rivalry

  • Intensity of competition among existing firms
  • High rivalry = price wars, low profits

🆕 New Entrants

  • Threat of new companies entering the market
  • Barriers to entry reduce this threat

🔄 Substitutes

  • Alternative products that meet same need
  • High substitutes = lower pricing power

🛒 Buyer Power

  • How much influence customers have on price
  • High power = customers demand low prices

■ TOPIC 1 — Introduction to Marketing
Q1
Define marketing and explain its role in an organisation.
▾
Definition: Marketing is the process of planning and executing the conception, pricing, promotion, and distribution of ideas, goods, and services to create exchanges that satisfy individual and organisational goals (AMA definition). Kotler describes it as "a social and managerial process by which individuals and groups obtain what they need and want through creating, offering, and exchanging products of value."

Role in an organisation:
  • Identifies customer needs and wants through research
  • Develops products/services that satisfy those needs
  • Sets prices that reflect value and generate profit
  • Distributes products through appropriate channels
  • Communicates value to target markets through promotion
  • Builds long-term relationships with customers
  • Drives revenue, brand equity, and competitive advantage
Q2
Describe the five marketing management philosophies/concepts.
▾
  • Production Concept: Consumers prefer products that are widely available and affordable. Companies focus on production efficiency and distribution. Works when demand exceeds supply.
  • Product Concept: Consumers favour products offering the most quality, performance, and innovative features. Risk: marketing myopia — ignoring customer needs.
  • Selling Concept: Consumers won't buy enough unless the company undertakes aggressive selling and promotion. Focus is on transactions, not relationships.
  • Marketing Concept: Achieving organisational goals depends on knowing the needs/wants of target markets and delivering desired satisfactions better than competitors. Customer-first approach.
  • Societal Marketing Concept: The company should determine needs/wants of target markets and deliver satisfactions in a way that maintains/improves both consumer and society's wellbeing. Adds a social/ethical dimension.
Q3
Explain the evolution of marketing from the production era to the digital era.
▾
  • Production Era (pre-1920s): Demand exceeded supply. Companies focused on increasing production and reducing costs. "Build it and they will buy it."
  • Sales Era (1920s–1950s): Supply began to exceed demand. Companies adopted aggressive selling and advertising to move products. Customer needs still ignored.
  • Marketing Era (1950s–1980s): Customer needs placed at the centre. Companies discovered that understanding customer needs first leads to greater success. Marketing departments grew in power.
  • Relationship Marketing Era (1980s–2000s): Focus shifted from single transactions to long-term customer relationships. CRM systems emerged. Customer lifetime value became important.
  • Digital/Social Era (2000s–present): Internet, social media, big data, and mobile technology transformed marketing. Personalisation, two-way communication, influencer marketing, and e-commerce became dominant.
Q4
What are the 4 Ps of the Marketing Mix? Briefly explain each.
▾
The Marketing Mix (4 Ps) is the set of controllable tactical marketing tools that a firm blends to produce the response it wants in the target market:
  • Product: The goods, services, or ideas offered to satisfy customer needs. Includes design, quality, features, branding, packaging, and product lifecycle management.
  • Price: The amount customers pay. The only revenue-generating P. Includes pricing strategies (skimming, penetration), discounts, and payment terms.
  • Place (Distribution): How the product reaches the customer. Includes distribution channels, logistics, retail locations, and supply chain management.
  • Promotion: How the company communicates with customers. Includes advertising, sales promotion, public relations, personal selling, and direct marketing.
■ TOPIC 2 — Marketing Management
Q5
What is a Marketing Information System (MIS) and what are its four components?
▾
A Marketing Information System (MIS) consists of people, equipment, and procedures to gather, sort, analyse, evaluate, and distribute needed, timely, and accurate information to marketing decision makers.

Four components:
  • Internal Records System: Data collected within the company — orders, sales, prices, inventory, accounts receivable. Fastest way to get data.
  • Marketing Intelligence System: Everyday information about developments in the marketing environment — reading newspapers, talking to customers, suppliers, and distributors.
  • Marketing Research System: Systematic design, collection, analysis, and reporting of data relevant to a specific marketing situation. Can be primary (new) or secondary (existing) data.
  • Decision Support System (DSS): Coordinated collection of data, systems, tools, and techniques that help managers make better decisions. Uses models and software.
Q6
What is customer value and how is customer satisfaction measured?
▾
Customer Value is the difference between the total customer value (the bundle of benefits customers expect from a product) and the total customer cost (what they sacrifice to acquire it).

Customer Value = Total Benefits – Total Costs

Customer Satisfaction is the level of a person's felt state resulting from comparing a product's perceived performance with their expectations:

  • Performance < Expectations → Dissatisfied customer
  • Performance = Expectations → Satisfied customer
  • Performance > Expectations → Delighted customer
Measurement tools: Customer surveys, mystery shoppers, lost customer analysis, complaint tracking, Net Promoter Score (NPS).
Q7
Explain the marketing planning process using SWOT analysis.
▾
The marketing planning process involves:

1. Situation Analysis (SWOT):
  • Strengths (internal): What the company does well — strong brand, skilled staff, efficient processes
  • Weaknesses (internal): Areas needing improvement — limited budget, poor distribution
  • Opportunities (external): Favourable trends — new markets, changing customer preferences
  • Threats (external): Unfavourable forces — new competitors, regulations, economic downturns
2. Set Marketing Objectives: SMART goals based on SWOT findings.
3. Develop Strategies: How to achieve objectives using the 4 Ps.
4. Implement Action Plans: Assign responsibilities, timelines, budgets.
5. Monitor and Control: Track performance and make corrections.
■ TOPIC 3 — Product Decisions
Q8
Explain Kotler's three levels of a product with examples.
▾
1. Core Product (Core Benefit):
The fundamental service or benefit the customer is really buying. It answers: "What is the buyer really buying?"
Example: When buying a hotel room, the core product is rest and sleep.

2. Actual Product:
The physical product with specific features, quality level, brand name, design, and packaging that delivers the core benefit.
Example: The hotel room itself — the bed, decor, cleanliness, brand (e.g., Hilton), and room features.

3. Augmented Product:
Additional services and benefits built around the core and actual product — warranties, after-sales service, delivery, installation, customer support.
Example: Free breakfast, airport shuttle, loyalty points, 24/7 room service at the hotel.
Q9
What are the stages of the New Product Development process?
▾
  • 1. Idea Generation: Systematic search for new product ideas — from customers, employees, R&D, competitors.
  • 2. Idea Screening: Filtering to identify good ideas and drop poor ones early. Uses criteria like market size, profitability, feasibility.
  • 3. Concept Development & Testing: Develop detailed concept and test with target consumers to gauge interest and understanding.
  • 4. Marketing Strategy Development: Design initial marketing strategy for new product.
  • 5. Business Analysis: Review of sales, costs, and profit projections to assess viability.
  • 6. Product Development: R&D or engineering creates the physical prototype.
  • 7. Test Marketing: Product introduced into a realistic market setting to test marketing programme.
  • 8. Commercialisation: Full-scale launch — when, where, to whom, and how.
Q10
What is brand equity and why is it important?
▾
Brand Equity is the positive differential effect that knowing the brand name has on customer response to the product or service. It represents the added value a brand gives to a product beyond its functional benefits.

Sources of Brand Equity:
  • Brand awareness — how well-known is the brand
  • Perceived quality — customer perception of quality
  • Brand associations — mental connections linked to the brand
  • Brand loyalty — repeat purchases and advocacy
Why it matters: High brand equity means customers will pay premium prices, be more loyal, and recommend the brand. It provides competitive advantage, makes it easier to launch new products, and has significant financial value (Apple's brand is worth over $880 billion).
Q11
Describe the Product Lifecycle and suggest marketing strategies for each stage.
▾
  • Introduction: Low sales, high costs, no profits yet. Strategy: Create awareness, target early adopters, use penetration or skimming pricing. Heavy advertising investment.
  • Growth: Rapidly rising sales, improving profits, new competitors enter. Strategy: Maximise market share, improve quality, enter new segments, add distribution channels.
  • Maturity: Sales peak, profits decline due to competition. Strategy: Defend market share, differentiate, modify product, run promotions, cut prices selectively.
  • Decline: Sales fall, profits collapse. Strategy: Reduce costs, harvest the product, or exit the market. Focus resources on newer products.
■ TOPIC 4 — Consumer Behaviour & Segmentation
Q12
What are the four major factors that influence consumer buying behaviour?
▾
  • Cultural Factors: Culture (values, norms, beliefs of society), subculture (nationality, religion, racial groups), and social class (income, education, occupation).
  • Social Factors: Reference groups (friends, celebrities, family), family roles, and social status. Family is the most important buying organisation — husband/wife influence varies by product category.
  • Personal Factors: Age and life cycle stage, occupation, economic situation, lifestyle (activities, interests, opinions), and personality/self-concept.
  • Psychological Factors: Motivation (Maslow's hierarchy), perception (how we interpret stimuli), learning (changes from experience), and beliefs/attitudes (predispositions to act).
Q13
What is market segmentation? Explain the four bases of segmentation.
▾
Market Segmentation is the process of dividing a market into distinct groups of buyers who have different needs, characteristics, or behaviours, and who might require separate products or marketing programmes.

Four Bases:
  • Geographic: Divide market by nations, regions, cities, or neighbourhoods. Example: Urban vs rural marketing.
  • Demographic: Age, gender, income, education, occupation, family size, religion, nationality. Most popular basis because needs and buying patterns vary closely with demographic variables.
  • Psychographic: Lifestyle (activities, interests, opinions), personality, social class, values. Example: Luxury goods target status-conscious consumers.
  • Behavioural: Occasions, usage rate (heavy/light), benefits sought, loyalty status, readiness stage. Example: Airlines target frequent flyers differently from occasional travellers.
Q14
Explain Maslow's Hierarchy of Needs and its relevance to marketing.
▾
Maslow's Hierarchy proposes that human needs are arranged in a pyramid with five levels. People must satisfy lower-level needs before seeking higher-level ones:

  • Physiological: Food, water, shelter, sleep. Marketing: basic food brands, utility products.
  • Safety: Security, protection from harm. Marketing: insurance, home security, health products.
  • Social: Belonging, love, friendship. Marketing: social media, beverages/food ("share a Coke"), clothing brands.
  • Esteem: Self-esteem, recognition, prestige. Marketing: luxury cars, premium brands, award programmes.
  • Self-Actualisation: Self-fulfilment, achieving potential. Marketing: education, travel, creative tools.
Relevance: Marketers use the hierarchy to understand what motivates their target market and position their products accordingly.
Q15
What are the four targeting strategies? When should each be used?
▾
  • Undifferentiated (Mass) Marketing: One offer for the entire market. Use when product appeals to all (e.g., basic commodities, salt). Lowest cost, but ignores diversity.
  • Differentiated Marketing: Different offers for multiple segments. Use when company has resources to tailor programmes. Higher cost but potentially higher sales (e.g., car manufacturers offering different models).
  • Concentrated (Niche) Marketing: One large share of one or few sub-markets. Use for smaller companies with limited resources targeting a specific need gap. High risk if segment shrinks.
  • Micromarketing (Local/Individual): Tailoring products for local or individual customer needs. Use with big data and personalisation technology. Most expensive but highest relevance (e.g., Amazon product recommendations).
■ TOPIC 6 — Pricing Strategies
Q16
What are the key steps involved in setting a price?
▾
  • Step 1 – Select the Pricing Objective: Survival, maximum profit, maximum market share, or product quality leadership.
  • Step 2 – Determine Demand: Understand price elasticity. Elastic demand = price-sensitive customers. Inelastic = price insensitive.
  • Step 3 – Estimate Costs: Calculate fixed costs (don't vary with output) and variable costs (vary with output). Total cost = Fixed + Variable.
  • Step 4 – Analyse Competitors' Costs, Prices, and Offers: Know where your price sits relative to competition and what value you offer by comparison.
  • Step 5 – Select a Pricing Method: Cost-plus, target-return, perceived-value, going-rate, or auction-type pricing.
  • Step 6 – Select the Final Price: Consider psychological pricing (e.g., K99 instead of K100), company pricing policies, and the impact on other parties (distributors, salespeople).
Q17
Differentiate between penetration pricing and market skimming pricing. Give examples.
▾
Market Penetration Pricing:
  • Setting a low initial price to attract a large number of buyers quickly and gain market share
  • Conditions needed: price-sensitive market, economies of scale available, low price discourages competition
  • Example: Netflix entering a market at a low subscription price; new mobile network operators with cheap data
Market Skimming Pricing:
  • Setting a high initial price to skim maximum revenue from segments willing to pay the high price
  • Conditions needed: sufficient buyers with high current demand, product perceived as high quality/unique, high price signals prestige
  • Example: Apple launching new iPhone at premium price; new PlayStation console launch pricing
Key difference: Penetration builds volume fast at low margins; skimming builds profit early at low volume.
Q18
What is price elasticity and why does it matter for pricing decisions?
▾
Price Elasticity of Demand measures how sensitive customer demand is to a change in price:

Price Elasticity = % Change in Quantity Demanded ÷ % Change in Price

  • Elastic Demand (|e| > 1): A small price increase causes a large drop in demand. Customers are very price-sensitive. Example: luxury goods, branded clothing.
  • Inelastic Demand (|e| < 1): A price change causes little change in demand. Customers are not price-sensitive. Example: insulin for diabetics, basic utilities.
  • Unitary Elastic (|e| = 1): A 1% price change leads to a 1% change in demand.
Why it matters: If demand is inelastic, you can raise prices to increase revenue. If elastic, raising prices will reduce revenue. Firms must know their elasticity before changing prices.
■ TOPIC 7 — Promotion & Communications Mix
Q19
What are the five tools of the Marketing Communications Mix? Explain each briefly.
▾
  • Advertising: Any paid form of non-personal presentation and promotion of ideas, goods, or services by an identified sponsor. Examples: TV ads, billboards, online banner ads. Reaches large audiences but is one-directional.
  • Sales Promotion: Short-term incentives to encourage purchase or trial of a product. Examples: coupons, free samples, buy-one-get-one, contests, loyalty points.
  • Public Relations (PR): Building good relations with various publics through favourable publicity, building a good corporate image, and handling unfavourable events. Examples: press releases, sponsorships, events.
  • Personal Selling: Personal customer presentations by the firm's sales force for the purpose of making sales and building customer relationships. Most effective but most expensive per contact.
  • Direct Marketing: Connecting directly with carefully targeted individual consumers — phone, mail, email, online. Examples: email campaigns, direct mail catalogues, SMS marketing.
Q20
Describe the four methods used to set the advertising budget.
▾
  • Affordable Method: Set the budget at what management thinks the company can afford after all other expenses. Problem: ignores effect of advertising on sales and may underfund during growth periods.
  • Percentage-of-Sales Method: Set budget as a fixed percentage of current or forecasted sales. Simple and creates relationship between ad spending and performance. Problem: treats advertising as a result of sales rather than a cause.
  • Competitive Parity Method: Match competitors' advertising spending. Uses industry benchmarks. Prevents advertising wars. Problem: assumes competitors' budgets are correct for your situation.
  • Objective-and-Task Method: Most logical method. Define specific objectives, determine tasks needed to achieve them, and estimate the cost. Budget = cost of all tasks needed. Problem: requires knowing what tasks are needed and their cost.
Q21
What is Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC) and why is it important?
▾
Integrated Marketing Communications (IMC) is the concept under which a company carefully integrates and coordinates its many communications channels to deliver a clear, consistent, and compelling message about the organisation and its products.

Why IMC matters:
  • Customers receive messages from many sources — TV, social media, salespeople, packaging. IMC ensures they all say the same thing.
  • Inconsistent messages create confusion and damage brand credibility.
  • IMC maximises communication impact through synergy — the total effect is greater than individual parts.
  • Helps build stronger brand identity and trust.
  • More cost-effective when all tools work together toward the same objectives.
Example: A Coca-Cola Christmas campaign uses TV ads, billboards, social media, in-store promotions, and packaging all featuring the same theme and message.
■ TOPIC 8 — Managing the Sales Force
Q22
What are the main structures used to organise a sales force?
▾
  • Territorial Sales Force Structure: Each sales rep is assigned an exclusive geographic area and sells the company's full product line in that territory. Simple, defines rep's responsibilities clearly, and reduces travel costs.
  • Product Sales Force Structure: Sales force is organised along product lines. Used when products are technically complex or numerous. Disadvantage: multiple reps visiting same customer.
  • Customer (Market) Sales Force Structure: Organised by type of customer or industry. Allows reps to develop deep customer knowledge. Example: Separate teams for government, corporate, and small business clients.
  • Complex/Combination Structure: Combines two or more of the above structures. Used by large companies selling many products to many types of customers across many regions.
Size: Determined by the workload approach — number of accounts × call frequency × call duration.
Q23
What methods can companies use to motivate their sales force?
▾
Financial Motivators:
  • Straight salary — stable income, low motivation to sell more
  • Straight commission — high motivation but unstable income
  • Salary plus commission — most common combination
  • Bonuses for reaching quotas or exceeding targets
  • Benefits: car allowance, health insurance, expense accounts
Non-Financial Motivators:
  • Recognition — "Salesperson of the Month" awards, public acknowledgement
  • Career development — promotions, training opportunities
  • Sales contests — create excitement and friendly competition
  • Autonomy — allowing reps to manage their own territories
  • Clear, fair, and challenging quotas — a sense of achievement when met
Q24
Explain the 7-step personal selling process.
▾
  • 1. Prospecting & Qualifying: Identify potential customers (prospects) and assess whether they have a need, ability to pay, and authority to buy.
  • 2. Pre-approach: Learn as much as possible about the prospect before the meeting — their needs, buying process, and key decision-makers.
  • 3. Approach: Make the first contact with the customer. Create a good first impression. Open with a question or demonstration to capture interest.
  • 4. Presentation & Demonstration: Tell the product's story to the buyer — features, advantages, and benefits. Show how the product solves the buyer's problem.
  • 5. Handling Objections: Seek out, clarify, and overcome customer objections. Use a positive, logical approach. View objections as opportunities to provide more information.
  • 6. Closing: Ask for the order. Look for closing signals — customer asks about delivery or price. Use techniques: trial close, assumptive close, urgency close.
  • 7. Follow-up: Follow up after the sale to ensure customer satisfaction and repeat business. Resolve any issues promptly.
■ TOPIC 9 — Dealing with Competition
Q25
Distinguish between the industry concept and the market concept of competition.
▾
Industry Concept of Competition:
  • A group of firms that offer a product or class of products that are close substitutes for each other
  • Narrow view — focuses only on direct competitors in the same industry
  • Example: Zambia Breweries might only view other beer producers as competitors
  • Risk: Leads to marketing myopia — you miss broader competitive threats
Market Concept of Competition:
  • All companies that compete for the same customer need or money
  • Broader, more customer-focused view of who competes with you
  • Example: Zambia Breweries also competes with soft drink makers, water brands, juice companies — all vying for the same customer's entertainment/refreshment budget
  • Advantage: Spots threats early and reveals new opportunities
Q26
Explain Porter's Five Forces model and its use in competitive analysis.
▾
Porter's Five Forces is a framework for analysing the competitive environment of an industry and determining its profitability potential:

  • 1. Rivalry Among Existing Competitors: Intensity of competition. High when many similar competitors, slow growth, high fixed costs. Forces prices down and profit margins thin.
  • 2. Threat of New Entrants: New companies can enter and take market share. Reduced by barriers to entry — patents, capital requirements, brand loyalty, government regulations.
  • 3. Threat of Substitute Products: Alternative products that meet the same customer need. High substitutes = buyers have more options = lower pricing power for the firm.
  • 4. Bargaining Power of Buyers: Customers' ability to drive prices down. High when few large buyers, standard products, or buyers can easily switch.
  • 5. Bargaining Power of Suppliers: Suppliers' ability to drive input prices up. High when few suppliers, unique inputs, or switching costs are high.
Use: Helps companies identify which forces threaten profitability and develop strategies to protect or improve their position.
Q27
What are Porter's three generic competitive strategies?
▾
  • Cost Leadership: Achieve the lowest cost of production in the industry while maintaining acceptable quality and profit margins. Sell at market price but with higher margins, OR undercut competitors. Example: Walmart, Shoprite. Requires: tight cost controls, efficient processes, economies of scale.
  • Differentiation: Offer something unique that customers value and will pay a premium for. Could be product features, brand image, quality, technology, or service. Example: Apple, BMW. Requires: strong R&D, creativity, strong marketing, customer loyalty.
  • Focus (Niche): Concentrate on a narrow target segment and serve it better than anyone else — either through low cost or differentiation within that niche. Example: Rolls-Royce (luxury niche), a local organic bakery. Requires: deep understanding of the niche segment's needs.
Warning: Being "stuck in the middle" — trying to be everything to everyone — usually leads to failure.
Q28
What are the four competitive positions in a market and what strategy should each adopt?
▾
  • Market Leader (largest share): Expand total market, protect current market share (defensive strategies), and expand market share. Example: Coca-Cola in soft drinks.
  • Market Challenger (#2 or #3): Aggressively attack the market leader or other rivals to gain market share. Strategies: frontal attack (compete directly), flanking attack (attack weaknesses), guerrilla attack. Example: Pepsi vs Coca-Cola.
  • Market Follower: Copy the leader, avoiding risky confrontation. Maintain current customers, grow through acquisition of smaller rivals. Lower risk, lower reward. Example: Many regional beverage brands.
  • Market Nicher: Serve one or more small segments the large players ignore. Achieve high margins by specialising deeply. Risk: segment may disappear or attract larger competitors. Example: specialty bookshops, artisan food producers.
■ BONUS — Cross-Topic Application Questions
Q29
A company is launching a new energy drink in Zambia. Walk through how they would apply the marketing mix (4 Ps).
▾
Product: Design a high-energy drink with a distinctive taste, appealing packaging, and a strong brand name (e.g., "Zamba Energy"). Include augmented features like a loyalty programme. Product should target young professionals and students aged 18–35.

Price: Use penetration pricing initially (e.g., K15 per can) to gain market share. Once established and brand equity builds, gradually increase price. Offer multi-pack discounts to encourage trial.

Place: Distribute through supermarkets (Shoprite, Pick n Pay), fuel stations, gyms, university campuses, and sports events. Prioritise Lusaka and Copperbelt first, then expand nationally.

Promotion: Launch with social media campaign (Instagram, TikTok), partner with Zambian sports influencers and athletes, sponsor local football matches, in-store sampling, and radio ads on stations popular with youth. Use IMC to ensure consistent messaging across all channels.
Q30
How has the rise of digital technology changed marketing practices? Give examples.
▾
Digital technology has fundamentally transformed marketing in several key ways:

  • From mass to personalised marketing: Big data allows companies to send individualised messages based on browsing history, purchase behaviour, and demographics. Example: Amazon's product recommendations.
  • Two-way communication: Social media enables customers to respond, share, and co-create content. Companies can engage in real-time conversations. Example: Brands responding to customer tweets.
  • Measurability: Digital marketing provides precise metrics — clicks, conversion rates, cost per acquisition. Traditional advertising couldn't measure impact this precisely.
  • Lower barriers for small businesses: Small businesses in Zambia can compete nationally using Facebook Ads, WhatsApp Business, and Instagram at low cost.
  • E-commerce: Online stores have disrupted traditional retail. Consumers can buy from anywhere at any time.
  • Influencer marketing: Social media influencers have replaced traditional celebrity endorsements for reaching niche audiences cost-effectively.
  • Mobile-first: Marketing must be optimised for mobile devices, particularly in Africa where mobile penetration exceeds desktop usage.
